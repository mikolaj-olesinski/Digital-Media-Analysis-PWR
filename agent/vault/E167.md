# E167 · Reverse-engineering the visible distribution: follower vs reaction elasticity

## Question and hypothesis
- Question: from the data alone, how much does a post's visibility depend on the author's follower base versus its
  reactions, and does that differ by platform?
- Hypothesis: follower elasticity is lowest on TikTok (feed distribution) and highest on YouTube (subscriptions).
- Refuted if: the elasticities are the same within the bootstrap CI.

## Setup
- Per platform, OLS on log10(views): `a * log10(followers+1) + b * log10(1 + likes + comments + shares) + c`.
- Brand-grouped 5-fold OOF R2; 200 brand-bootstrap resamples for the CIs.
- Amplification = views / followers (median, p90, share > 1).
- Run: `python src/platform_law.py`.

## Result
| platform | follower elasticity (95% CI) | reaction elasticity (95% CI) | OOF R2 | median views/follower | share > followers |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| TikTok | **0.044** (0.028-0.063) | 0.823 (0.798-0.842) | 0.812 | 0.61 | **43.4%** |
| YouTube | **0.303** (0.272-0.347) | 0.660 (0.615-0.711) | 0.587 | 0.02 | 5.1% |
| X | **0.164** (0.128-0.200) | **1.060** (0.994-1.128) | 0.470 | 0.06 | 13.5% |

## Decision
The platforms have visibly different distribution laws. On TikTok the follower base explains almost nothing (elasticity
0.04) and 43% of posts get more views than the author has followers: visibility is reaction-driven, i.e. feed
distribution. On YouTube the subscriber base matters three times more (0.30) and only 5% of posts beat the follower
count. X sits between (0.16) but its reaction elasticity is about 1: views grow proportionally with reactions. This is
consistent with the model (reactions carry the signal) and explains why a follower-based rule is structurally wrong on
TikTok.
